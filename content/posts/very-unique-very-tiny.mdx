I was talking to Dad the other day, and he tried rules-lawyering the whole family's use of "unique", arguing that something either is unique or it isn't (ergo "very unique" isn't a valid combo). It was an interesting enough dispute for me to look into… and it turns out he's right, but he's also not. It's contentious enough that [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/grammar/very-unique-and-absolute-adjectives) has an article on the debate.

Basically, there are certain adjectives that people in the 1800s tried to class as "absolute adjectives": either something is that thing or it isn't. Take "square". An object is either square or not square, and there's no spectrum between the two. That falls apart in practice, where things can be almost square (close enough to be called square), and then "very" becomes a useful modifier. It's a similar case for "unique", which historically meant there's only one. Through hyperbolic use, a more common meaning has emerged, "uncommon" or "non-standard" rather than truly one of a kind, and that newer meaning takes "very" or other modifiers just fine.

One last interesting thing I found (I promise): "non-gradable extreme" adjectives, whose usage also gets policed. "Freezing" already means "very cold", so "very freezing" sounds weird and isn't quite right. One thing I found funny is that IELTS (the English test often used for immigration) [seems to care about this](https://ielts.idp.com/nepal/about/news-and-articles/article-adjectives-gradable-non-gradable), though I'm not sure it would actually be tested. It lumps them in with the absolute adjectives above.

The linguist [Marcin Morzycki](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11049-011-9162-0) puts "tiny" in the same camp as "freezing", though "very tiny" doesn't set off any alarm bells for me. Maybe that's because "very" and "tiny" half-rhyme, both ending in "-y" (or for some other reason; I'm just guessing). He also notes that which adjectives count as extreme varies a lot from person to person.
